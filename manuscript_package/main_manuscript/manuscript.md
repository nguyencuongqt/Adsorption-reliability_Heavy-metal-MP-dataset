# Grouped Validation and Null-Feature Audits Reveal Reliability Limits in Literature-Derived Models of Heavy-Metal Adsorption by Microplastics

## Abstract

Machine learning on literature-derived environmental data is often evaluated with row-level validation and interpreted with a single feature-importance method, even when the data are strongly grouped and heterogeneous. Here we studied reliability of both prediction and interpretation for a compiled dataset of heavy-metal adsorption by microplastics containing 1009 observations from 149 experiments nested within 23 studies. We framed two research questions: how grouped validation changes estimated predictive performance, and how sensitive mechanistic conclusions are to the feature-importance method. Using the locked final dataset and the legacy high-capacity training pipeline, we compared random row-level cross-validation, grouped experiment-level validation, and grouped study-level validation. Descriptively, the multilayer perceptron (`MLP`) performed best under random cross-validation (`R2 = 0.912 +/- 0.039`, `RMSE = 0.618 +/- 0.118`), whereas the Light Gradient Boosting Machine (`LGBM`) gave the strongest experiment-level transfer (`R2 = 0.725 +/- 0.203`, `RMSE = 0.969 +/- 0.387`). Study-level grouped validation remained difficult for all tested models, with all `group_aut` summaries showing markedly weaker and more variable performance than `random_cv`. For interpretation, we compared standardized Elastic Net (`EN`) coefficients, permutation importance, `LGBM` built-in gain, and SHAP, while augmenting the dataset with synthetic null features. Importance rankings differed materially by method, and null-feature benchmarking showed a tradeoff between stability and susceptibility to spurious attribution rather than a single universally reliable method. These results show that random validation can be substantially optimistic in grouped literature-derived environmental data, and that interpretation reliability should be stress-tested rather than assumed from a single method.

**Keywords:** grouped validation; microplastics; heavy metals; adsorption; feature importance; SHAP; null features; interpretation reliability

## 1. Introduction

Microplastics are persistent anthropogenic particles that are increasingly recognized as reactive contaminant carriers rather than inert debris. In addition to sorbing organic compounds, microplastics can adsorb dissolved trace metals, and the extent of uptake depends on polymer chemistry, surface aging, pH, ionic strength, and metal identity or speciation (Thompson et al., 2004, doi:10.1126/science.1094559; Turner and Holmes, 2015, doi:10.1071/EN14143; Tang et al., 2020, doi:10.1016/j.jhazmat.2019.121960). Recent synthesis confirms that adsorption capacity is not a single material constant but varies systematically across microplastic types, metals, and experimental environments, making pooled literature datasets intrinsically heterogeneous (Zhu et al., 2023, doi:10.1007/s11356-023-28131-6; Bi et al., 2024, doi:10.1016/j.envpol.2024.124634).

This heterogeneity has made machine learning attractive for environmental adsorption modeling because ML can integrate nonlinear responses across materials and solution conditions. In environmental research more broadly, however, predictive performance is often overstated when validation ignores temporal, spatial, or hierarchical structure, or when leakage pathways are left unchecked (Roberts et al., 2017, doi:10.1111/ecog.02881; Zhu et al., 2023, doi:10.1021/acs.est.3c00026). The issue is directly relevant to microplastic adsorption studies: recent papers have used ML to predict adsorption and infer influential variables for xenobiotics, mixed heavy metals, or single-metal systems under varying environmental conditions (Bryant and Ma, 2023, doi:10.1021/acsestwater.3c00151; Wang et al., 2025, doi:10.1016/j.surfin.2025.107101; Gong et al., 2025, doi:10.3390/w17091316). Yet these datasets are commonly literature-derived or pooled across experiments, where rows are clustered within experiments and studies and therefore rarely satisfy the independent and identically distributed assumptions implicit in row-level validation.

Interpretation presents a second reliability problem. Feature-importance methods are useful for summarizing predictive relevance, but they are not direct tests of adsorption mechanism or causality, and their rankings can shift with model class, feature dependence, and explainer choice (Murdoch et al., 2019, doi:10.1073/pnas.1900654116; Molnar et al., 2023, doi:10.1007/s10618-022-00901-9; Yasodhara et al., 2021, doi:10.1007/978-3-030-84060-0_19). For heterogeneous environmental tabular data, chemically plausible narratives can therefore still rest on unstable or spuriously inflated importance scores. Despite growing use of SHAP, permutation importance, and model-specific gain scores in environmental ML, explicit reliability assessment of interpretability remains uncommon in this application area.

Against this background, the central gap is not the absence of predictive models, but the absence of joint reliability assessment for both prediction and interpretation in literature-derived microplastic-metal adsorption datasets. Here we address that gap by evaluating a compiled dataset of heavy-metal adsorption by microplastics under a validation hierarchy that respects grouped structure and by stress-testing interpretability with synthetic null features. The study's novelty is therefore twofold: first, it quantifies how apparent performance changes from random row-level validation to grouped experiment-level and study-level transfer; second, it tests whether commonly used feature-importance methods remain robust when confronted with deliberately non-informative predictors. Accordingly, `RQ1` examines how validation design under grouped literature-derived data changes estimated predictive performance and apparent generalization. `RQ2` examines how sensitive mechanistic conclusions are to the choice of feature-importance method, and whether null-feature stress testing can identify methods that appear stable yet remain vulnerable to spurious attribution.

### 1.1. Hypotheses

To operationalize `RQ1` and `RQ2`, we tested the following hypotheses.

- `H1`: Predictive performance estimated under `random_cv` will be systematically more optimistic than performance estimated under grouped validation, as indicated by higher `R2` and lower `RMSE` and `MAE` when the same models are evaluated across validation regimes.
- `H2`: Generalization difficulty will follow the validation hierarchy `random_cv` < `group_exp` < `group_aut`, such that transfer to unseen studies will yield the weakest and most variable predictive performance.
- `H3`: Model behavior will be regime-dependent rather than uniform across validation designs; specifically, higher-capacity nonlinear models will show stronger in-domain performance under `random_cv`, whereas their advantage will attenuate under grouped transfer relative to a simpler regularized baseline.
- `H4`: Mechanistic conclusions derived from feature importance will be method-sensitive, such that the relative ranking of major predictor groups will differ across coefficient magnitude, permutation importance, built-in gain, and SHAP when applied to the same locked dataset.
- `H5`: Synthetic null features will receive non-zero attribution from at least some importance methods, and null contamination will therefore be detectable through aggregate null importance, top-rank null intrusion, and real-versus-null separation diagnostics.
- `H6`: Ranking stability alone will not guarantee interpretive reliability; methods that appear stable across resampling or validation regimes will not necessarily be the methods that best suppress null features, implying a tradeoff among stability, conservatism, and susceptibility to spurious attribution.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1. Dataset and grouped structure

The study used a locked, literature-derived dataset assembled from a Scopus-based search using keyword combinations for microplastics, adsorption or sorption, and the target metals. After screening and harmonization, the final modeling dataset contained 1009 observations from 23 studies (`aut_id`) and 149 experiments (`exp_id`). The represented metals were Cd(II) (`n = 535`), Cr(VI) (`n = 308`), and Hg(II) (`n = 166`). Microplastic classes were encoded as PE, PP, PS, PVC, PET, PA, PLA, and other.

The response variable was adsorption capacity (`qe`, `mg g^-1`), defined as the equilibrium mass of metal adsorbed per unit mass of microplastic. The locked feature set contained 25 predictors grouped into solution conditions (`ph`, `temp`, `ce`, `rpm`), surface area (`sa`), surface-functionality indicators, aging-state indicators, metal-identity indicators, polymer-identity indicators, and missingness indicators for partially reported continuous variables. The `qe` distribution was strongly right-skewed (median `0.103`, interquartile range `0.032-0.454`, 95th percentile `5.052`, maximum `17.779` `mg g^-1`). Missingness was highest for `ph` (`53.3%`) and `sa` (`38.0%`), with lower missingness for `temp` (`10.3%`) and `rpm` (`5.4%`). Variable definitions, units, and missingness are summarized in `Table S1`.

### 2.2. Modeling target and preprocessing

The target was modeled as `log1p(qe)` to reduce the influence of extreme right-tail values while keeping evaluation on the original `qe` scale. All preprocessing steps that could leak information were fit inside training folds only. Specifically, continuous predictors were imputed with training-fold medians, and scaling was fit on training data only for models that required it. Validation rows were never used to fit imputers, scalers, or model parameters.

### 2.3. Validation hierarchy

We defined three validation regimes. `random_cv` corresponds to repeated row-level interpolation within the observed literature domain. `group_exp` uses grouped cross-validation by `exp_id` and represents transfer to unseen experiments while some study context remains represented in training. `group_aut` uses grouped cross-validation by `aut_id` and represents transfer to unseen studies.

### 2.4. Predictive models

We evaluated three model paradigms: a linear model, a tree-based ensemble, and a neural network. Elastic Net (EN) is a penalized linear regression model that combines `L1` and `L2` regularization, enabling coefficient shrinkage and variable selection under correlated predictors (Zou and Hastie, 2005, doi:10.1111/j.1467-9868.2005.00503.x). It was included as a sparse, interpretable baseline for grouped generalization.

Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LGBM) is a histogram-based gradient-boosted decision-tree algorithm that captures nonlinear effects and higher-order interactions through stage-wise tree boosting (Ke et al., 2017, doi:10.5555/3294996.3295074). We used it as the tree-based paradigm because adsorption responses may depend on threshold effects and interactions among solution conditions, polymer identity, and aging state that are not well represented by linear terms alone.

The multilayer perceptron (MLP) is a feed-forward neural network trained by back-propagation, allowing flexible nonlinear approximation through stacked hidden layers (Rumelhart et al., 1986, doi:10.1038/323533a0). It was used as a lightweight neural baseline to test whether higher capacity improves in-domain prediction while keeping the comparison focused on three distinct model families.

### 2.5. Feature-importance methods and null-feature stress testing

For `RQ2`, we compared four main importance methods: standardized coefficient magnitude for `EN`, permutation importance for both `EN` and `LGBM`, `LGBM` built-in gain, and SHAP for `LGBM`. We also added seven synthetic null features: pure random noise features, permuted versions of real features, and group-aware random-effect features keyed to studies and experiments but designed to remain non-informative about `qe`. We evaluated methods by null suppression, separation between real and null features, top-rank contamination by nulls, ranking stability across resampling, and robustness across validation regimes. These diagnostics were treated as evidence of interpretation reliability, not as universal proof that a method is correct.

## 3. Results and Discussion

### 3.1. Dataset structure confirms a grouped and heterogeneous prediction problem

The final dataset defines a genuinely grouped prediction problem rather than a conventional independent-row regression task. As summarized in `Table 1`, the 1009 observations are nested within only 23 studies and 149 experiments, with a median of 38 rows per study and 6 rows per experiment, and with clear concentration of observations in a small number of source studies. The response distribution is also highly uneven: median `qe` was `0.103 mg g^-1`, the 95th percentile was `5.052 mg g^-1`, and the maximum reached `17.779 mg g^-1` (`Table 1`). Substantial missingness in key covariates, especially `ph` (`53.3%`) and `sa` (`38.0%`), adds a second layer of heterogeneity that is typical of pooled literature-derived environmental datasets (`Table 1`; `Table S1`).

This structure has direct methodological consequences for both prediction and interpretation. Rows drawn from the same study or experiment are unlikely to be statistically exchangeable because they share protocols, analytical conditions, reporting conventions, and often correlated chemistry domains; random row-level splitting can therefore preserve study fingerprints across train and validation folds and inflate apparent generalization. That logic is consistent with the broader ecological and environmental ML literature, which has shown that ignoring grouped structure or blocked dependence can lead to systematically optimistic performance estimates (Roberts et al., 2017, doi:10.1111/ecog.02881; Zhu et al., 2023, doi:10.1021/acs.est.3c00026). At the same time, the strong skewness and mixed reporting quality are scientifically plausible for microplastic-metal adsorption, where uptake depends strongly on pH, concentration, polymer state, and surface alteration rather than on a single universal material constant (Tang et al., 2020, doi:10.1016/j.jhazmat.2019.121960; Bi et al., 2024, doi:10.1016/j.envpol.2024.124634). Taken together, these properties justify treating the dataset as a reliability stress test for grouped environmental ML rather than as a benchmark for maximizing a single headline accuracy value.

### 3.2. Random validation is optimistic relative to grouped validation

Validation design changed the apparent predictive success of the models in a systematic way, consistent with `H1` and `H2` (`Table 2`; `Figure 1A`). Under `random_cv`, the strongest result came from `MLP` (`R2 = 0.912 +/- 0.039`, `RMSE = 0.618 +/- 0.118`, `MAE = 0.223 +/- 0.038`), with `LGBM` also performing strongly (`R2 = 0.871 +/- 0.070`, `RMSE = 0.762 +/- 0.224`). When the evaluation moved to unseen experiments, however, the best model was `LGBM`, and performance weakened to `R2 = 0.725 +/- 0.203` and `RMSE = 0.969 +/- 0.387`, while `MLP` also degraded (`R2 = 0.703 +/- 0.120`, `RMSE = 1.097 +/- 0.537`) (`Table 2`; `Figure 1A`). Under study-level transfer, all models deteriorated sharply: `EN` had the lowest mean `RMSE` (`1.674 +/- 1.671`), `LGBM` reached `1.906 +/- 1.460`, and `MLP` became extremely unstable (`RMSE = 19.481 +/- 22.380`) (`Table 2`; `Figure 1A`).

The generalization-gap analysis clarifies that this is not a minor calibration issue but a large shift in what the metrics mean scientifically (`Table 3`; `Figure 1B`). Relative to random row-level validation, `LGBM` incurred an `RMSE` penalty of `+0.206` under `group_exp` and `+1.143` under `group_aut`, whereas `MLP` incurred `+0.479` and `+18.862`, respectively (`Table 3`). Expressed relative to each model's `random_cv` baseline, this corresponds to an approximately `1.3x` and `2.5x` RMSE increase for `LGBM`, versus `1.8x` and `31.5x` for `MLP` (`Figure 1B`). Even though `EN` was weak under interpolation, its grouped-study `RMSE` became comparatively less catastrophic, which underscores that the hierarchy `random_cv -> group_exp -> group_aut` is primarily a hierarchy of transfer difficulty rather than a simple leaderboard reshuffle. This pattern agrees with the broader literature on structured validation, where blocked or grouped resampling typically yields more conservative but more realistic estimates of model portability than naive random splitting (Roberts et al., 2017, doi:10.1111/ecog.02881; Zhu et al., 2023, doi:10.1021/acs.est.3c00026). The main contribution here is to demonstrate that the same optimism problem persists in literature-derived microplastic adsorption data, where study-level transfer remains the dominant barrier even after restoring a stronger legacy training pipeline.

### 3.3. Different model classes respond differently to grouped transfer

Model ranking was strongly regime-dependent rather than fixed, which supports `H3` and argues against a single universally best model (`Table 2`; `Table 3`; `Figure 1A`; `Figure 1B`). `MLP` was the strongest interpolator under `random_cv`, but it lost that advantage once the validation target shifted from within-domain interpolation to grouped transfer; by `group_aut`, its mean `R2` collapsed to `-21744.538` with very large dispersion, and its `RMSE` rose to approximately `31.5x` its `random_cv` baseline, indicating that high-capacity nonlinear fitting did not translate into reliable study-level portability (`Table 2`; `Figure 1B`). `LGBM` occupied an intermediate position, retaining the strongest performance under `group_exp` while deteriorating substantially at the study level, where its `RMSE` reached about `2.5x` the `random_cv` baseline (`Figure 1B`). `EN`, by contrast, was never the best in-domain model, but it produced the lowest mean `RMSE` under the harshest grouped regime (`1.674 +/- 1.671`) and the smallest performance collapse among the three models in that setting (`Table 2`; `Table 3`; `Figure 1B`).

These differences are scientifically plausible because the three paradigms absorb structure in different ways. The strong random-split performance of `MLP` suggests that the legacy feature-engineering and preprocessing pipeline can exploit rich local regularities when train and validation samples remain close in covariate space, but the grouped-study collapse indicates that much of that apparent signal is not portable across study boundaries. `LGBM` appears better suited to moderate transfer because boosted trees can capture threshold-like and interaction-driven adsorption responses without requiring the same degree of smooth extrapolation, whereas the sparse `EN` solution behaves more conservatively when the test domain shifts substantially. The sub-unit `EN` ratios in `Figure 1B` should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that grouped transfer improved the model; rather, they reflect that `EN` was already weak under `random_cv`, so its absolute `RMSE` changed relatively little while the nonlinear models degraded much more sharply. This interpretation also helps reconcile the present results with recent adsorption ML studies, which often reported good nonlinear predictive performance under conventional validation schemes but rarely tested whether that advantage survives grouped transfer (Bryant and Ma, 2023, doi:10.1021/acsestwater.3c00151; Wang et al., 2025, doi:10.1016/j.surfin.2025.107101). The key takeaway is therefore not that simple models are intrinsically superior, but that model choice must be matched to the intended transfer regime and not inferred solely from random-split metrics.

### 3.4. Mechanistic conclusions are method-sensitive

The interpretation results show that broad mechanism narratives are more reproducible than detailed rank claims, which supports `H4` (`Figure 2`; `Table S6`). Across all methods, solution-chemistry terms remained consistently important, but the extent of that dominance varied sharply with the explainer. `EN` coefficient magnitude assigned essentially all displayed mechanism-group importance to solution chemistry (`0.062` in all three regimes), and `EN` permutation also concentrated strongly on solution chemistry (`0.056-0.062`) with only very small residual weight on surface area or other groups (`Table S6`). In contrast, `LGBM` SHAP distributed importance across several chemically plausible groups, including `other` (`0.024-0.025` in `random_cv` and `group_exp`), surface area and porosity (`0.021-0.024`), solution chemistry (`0.020-0.021`), and polymer identity (`0.010-0.016`), while `LGBM` permutation shifted even more strongly toward `other`, aging, and polymer-related terms in some regimes (`Table S6`; `Figure 2`).

This pattern matters because it changes the mechanistic story a reader would tell from the same trained models. The persistence of solution chemistry as a leading group is chemically credible and agrees with experimental understanding that pH, dissolved concentration, and related solution controls are major determinants of metal uptake by microplastics (Tang et al., 2020, doi:10.1016/j.jhazmat.2019.121960; Bi et al., 2024, doi:10.1016/j.envpol.2024.124634). However, the large method-to-method shifts in aging, polymer, surface-area, and higher-order engineered groups are equally important, because they show that explanatory emphasis is partly a property of the interpretation method rather than only of the data-generating process. That observation aligns with the interpretability literature, which has warned that feature-importance rankings can be unstable under correlated predictors and different attribution rules (Murdoch et al., 2019, doi:10.1073/pnas.1900654116; Molnar et al., 2023, doi:10.1007/s10618-022-00901-9). The novel contribution of the present analysis is therefore not merely to rank features, but to show that in this microplastic-metal setting the most defensible conclusion is a robust broad signal for solution chemistry, whereas finer mechanistic hierarchies remain method-dependent and should be treated cautiously.

### 3.5. Null features reveal interpretation fragility

The corrected null benchmark shows that interpretation fragility cannot be reduced to a single null-share metric (`Figure 3`; `Table S5`). After normalization, mean null-importance share and top-10 null intrusion were near zero for all methods, so the main separation in `Figure 3` came instead from mean real-vs-null importance gap and rank stability. On those axes, `LGBM` gain performed best (`mean real-null gap ~ 0.007`, `rank stability ~ 0.943`), followed by `LGBM` SHAP (`0.006`, `0.781`), whereas `EN` coefficient magnitude was relatively stable (`0.746`) but showed essentially no real-null separation under this metric, reflecting its strongly conservative sparsity pattern (`Table S5`; `Figure 3`). Both permutation methods were markedly less stable, especially `EN` permutation (`~0.290`) and `LGBM` permutation (`~0.384`), indicating that their rankings were much more sensitive to resampling and validation regime (`Table S5`; `Figure 3`).

Scientifically, this means that evidence for `H5` was weaker under the corrected normalization than originally expected, because aggregate null uptake was not the main discriminator among methods. Support for `H6`, however, remained clear: no single diagnostic was sufficient, and methods that looked acceptable on one criterion could still look weak on another. `EN` coefficient magnitude was conservative but minimally separating; `LGBM` gain was highly stable and separating; SHAP occupied an intermediate position; permutation methods were comparatively unstable. This more nuanced picture is consistent with recent work arguing that interpretability reliability should be evaluated through multiple complementary diagnostics rather than by assuming that one popular explainer is intrinsically trustworthy (Molnar et al., 2023, doi:10.1007/s10618-022-00901-9; Yasodhara et al., 2021, doi:10.1007/978-3-030-84060-0_19). The practical implication is that null-feature stress testing adds value not because it identifies a universally correct method, but because it reveals which explanation methods are conservative, which are stable, and which require stronger caution before being translated into mechanistic claims.

## 4. Conclusion

This study reframed literature-derived adsorption modeling as a reliability problem rather than a best-model contest. Random row-level validation produced substantially stronger performance estimates than grouped experiment-level and grouped study-level validation, confirming that validation design strongly affects apparent predictive success. At the same time, mechanistic interpretation was shown to be method-sensitive, and null-feature stress testing exposed clear differences in how aggressively importance methods promote spurious signal. The broader implication is that reliable environmental ML requires stress-testing both prediction and interpretation. For literature-derived grouped data, importance should be reported as predictive relevance under explicit validation assumptions, not as direct causal evidence.
